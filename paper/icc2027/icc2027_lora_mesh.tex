\begin{abstract}
We compare the optional calibrated MeshEcho route-selection variant with stylized
Meshtastic-like managed flooding and MeshCore-like cached source routing
under a shared packet-level LoRa simulation. These are protocol-inspired
models, not runs of either project's firmware. On 20 repeated-pair fading
seeds (793 unicasts per policy), MeshEcho's source-ACK and destination
packet-delivery ratios are 0.624/0.633, versus 0.505/0.989 for
Meshtastic-like and 0.410/0.448 for MeshCore-like; aggregate transmission
airtime is 23.7, 42.4, and 19.5 s per run. Thus MeshEcho obtains more
source ACKs than both models in this conditioned workload, but flooding
delivers substantially more packets to destinations. On 20 separate,
unconditioned random-pair seeds (813 unicasts), Meshtastic-like exceeds
MeshEcho in both delivery metrics while using 38.9 versus 152.8 s airtime.
A PRR-product route score with matched recovery shows no clear ACK-delivery
difference from MeshEcho in either workload. The results identify a
workload-dependent protocol trade-off, not firmware-level dominance or a
distinct advantage for MeshEcho's route score.
\end{abstract}

LoRa mesh, Internet of Things, route selection, fading, packet delivery ratio,

\section{Introduction}
LoRa is attractive for low-power IoT because it combines long link budget
with inexpensive radios \cite{augustin2016study}. A star topology is
insufficient for obstructed or infrastructure-poor deployments, motivating
multi-hop extensions \cite{wong2024multihop,cotrim2020lorawanmesh,
centelles2021beyond}. Multi-hop forwarding is expensive: a low data rate
occupies the channel for a long time, while a flood multiplies collision
opportunities. A cached source route reduces redundancy, but one weak hop can
invalidate an otherwise useful path.

The practical choice is not only which cached route to rank. Managed flooding
can deliver DATA through several paths while spending more transmissions;
on-demand source routing spends discovery time and may fail before DATA
starts. MeshEcho combines on-demand route discovery with a modeled
weakest-hop packet-reception-ratio (PRR) score and a bounded route-miss
fallback. We ask how that complete policy compares with two established
LoRa mesh design families under identical simulated topology and traffic.
We distinguish delivery at the destination from receipt of a reverse ACK
at the source; collapsing them would hide an important flooding trade-off.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a same-trace, seed-paired comparison of calibrated MeshEcho with
Meshtastic-like and MeshCore-like behavior models on conditioned recurring
pairs and an unconditioned random-pair stress case;
\item separate ACK, destination-delivery, aggregate TX-airtime, and modeled
energy reporting, including an adverse workload where flooding dominates;
\item a matched-recovery PRR-product control that tests whether MeshEcho's
max-min score adds value beyond a familiar path-quality metric.
\end{itemize}
The cross-protocol baselines were added after the earlier MeshEcho holdout
had been examined. These comparisons are exploratory, not a new
prespecified holdout or an evaluation of production firmware.

\section{Related Work}
Prior ICC LoRa studies range from analytical and simulation work
\cite{tu2020coverage,afisiadis2020fer} to testbed-supported evaluation
\cite{to2018lorans3,rochester2020lsc}. Multihop surveys and implementations
describe flooding, route state, and timing costs
\cite{wong2024multihop,sole2022implementation,pueyo2024minimalistic}.
Meshtastic documents managed flooding for broadcasts and, since version 2.6,
learned next-hop routing for direct messages \cite{meshtasticdocs}.
MeshCore documents an initially flooded message whose delivery report can
establish a path \cite{meshcore}. Our simplified counterparts isolate
flooding and cached source-route behavior; neither reproduces those full
implementations. ETX provides the established expected-transmissions
reference \cite{decouto2003etx}; we use a matched-recovery PRR-product
score to test a closer route-ranking alternative.

\section{Protocol and Model}
\subsection{Network model}
Nodes are uniformly placed in a square and share one half-duplex LoRa
channel. Unless noted, packets have 32-byte payloads, 17 dBm transmit power,
125-kHz bandwidth, coding rate 4/5, capture threshold 6 dB, and SF7. Airtime
follows the standard LoRa symbol calculation \cite{semtech2013lora}.
Log-distance path loss with log-normal shadowing produces SNR; reception is a
logistic function of SNR margin, and overlapping packets are accepted only
when the desired signal passes capture. The simulator records collisions,
route-cache events, and discovery success. Total airtime is the sum of every
packet's TX duration, including control, DATA, ACK, and recovery packets;
overlapping transmissions are not de-duplicated into channel-busy time.
Energy is calculated from configured TX current and from an always-listening
assumption for non-transmitting nodes, not measured battery consumption.

\subsection{Routing policies}
The Meshtastic-like model floods each DATA packet with delayed rebroadcast,
duplicate suppression, and a reverse ACK along the DATA copy's realized
path. It does not implement Meshtastic 2.6+ direct-message next-hop learning,
retry details, or complete firmware semantics. Hence its reverse-path ACK
outcome is specific to this harness, especially when DATA reaches a
destination by multiple copies.

The MeshCore-like model sends a separate route request (RREQ), waits 2 s
for destination candidates, replies along the shortest candidate, and
caches the resulting source route for 600 s. It has no route-miss fallback
in this comparison. Real MeshCore instead initially floods the message and
uses a delivery report to establish a path; our RREQ/RREP abstraction is
not a firmware implementation. Both routed models use matched RREQ relay
timing when they have a request, but their later transmissions can change
interference and candidate exposure.

Hereafter, MeshEcho denotes the simulator's optional
``meshecho-calibrated'' variant, not its default ``meshecho''
CLI policy. This evaluated variant uses the same RREQ collection window
and cache lifetime.
For each successfully received RREQ hop $i$, its score maps observed SINR
to the simulator's predicted PRR $\hat p_i$. It selects the path maximizing
the weakest modeled hop PRR, with shorter paths breaking ties:
\begin{equation}
\hat p_i=f_{\rm sim}(\mathrm{SINR}_i),\quad
C_{\rm cal}=\min_i\hat p_i,\quad
C_{\rm prod}=\prod_i\hat p_i.
\end{equation}
The PRR-product comparator uses $C_{\rm prod}$ with MeshEcho's same TTL-2
route-miss fallback. These scores use model-inferred PRR from received
requests, not independently measured link PRR; failed requests are censored.
MeshEcho invokes the TTL-2 fallback only if discovery has no candidate.
Its default confidence threshold is zero, so a selected route does not
launch confidence-triggered duplicate forwarding. Timeout retransmission
is disabled for both scored policies. Comparing either with flooding or
MeshCore-like measures an entire policy bundle; only the matched-recovery
PRR-product row tests the score choice more narrowly.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Two workloads and evidence status}
Each run has 50 nodes, 600 s duration, SF7, 4-dB static shadowing,
independent reception randomness, and 6-dB temporal block fading every
60 s. There are no application broadcasts. The same topology, application
schedule, and per-seed trace hash are used for all four policies. Table
\ref{tab:params} separates the two workload strata; neither is a hardware
measurement.

In the recurring-pair case, four directed pairs per seed are chosen from a
shared analytical graph with edge PRR $\geq0.90$ and graph distance 2--4
hops. The graph is not supplied to any policy. Every pair has scheduled
flows in at least two fading blocks. The case passed a per-seed gate for a
complete pair pool, mean graph distance at least two, and at least 10\%
of direct links below 0.99 static PRR. Its 793 unicasts per policy estimate
behavior conditional on connected, repeatedly used pairs; the 51--70 seeds
were already inspected in an earlier MeshEcho-only analysis. Adding the two
protocol-family baselines to them is a post-hoc exploratory extension, not
a newly untouched holdout.

The separate random-pair case redraws source and destination for each
Poisson application flow in an 18-km square; a pair may recur by chance.
Its 813 unicasts per policy
span seeds 2001--2020. No graph-distance or route-feasibility gate is
applied, so some demands can be difficult or disconnected. The parameter
choice followed the earlier holdout inspection; these fresh seeds give a
robustness check, not a preregistered confirmatory test.

\subsection{Measurements and inference}
ACK PDR requires a source-received reverse-path ACK; destination PDR only
requires DATA arrival. Each protocol's seed-level PDR divides its ACK or
destination count by that seed's observed unicasts; the reported mean gives
each of the 20 seeds equal weight. For a policy difference $d_s$, we report
$\bar d\pm t_{.975,19}s_d/\sqrt{20}$, pairing by seed. Airtime and modeled TX/RX
energy are per 600-s run. Flows within a seed are not independent
replicates; intervals are nominal and no multiplicity-adjusted superiority
claim is made. Routed policies share a 2-s discovery window, 600-s route
TTL, and matched request relay timing. Their candidate sets can still
diverge after earlier forwarding decisions change interference.

\subsection{Recurring-pair trade-off}
On the four connected pairs per seed, MeshEcho exceeds Meshtastic-like by
0.119 in source-ACK PDR while using 18.7 s less aggregate TX airtime and
38.1 J less modeled energy. The destination metric reverses the delivery
ranking: Meshtastic-like delivers 784/793 DATA packets, but only 400/793
source ACKs return; MeshEcho delivers 498/793 and returns 490/793 ACKs.
The flood's reverse ACK rule can make this gap large in the simulator and
must not be read as a measured Meshtastic failure rate. MeshEcho exceeds
MeshCore-like by 0.214 ACK and 0.186 destination PDR at 4.22 s more
airtime and 8.63 J more modeled energy. It therefore does not dominate
MeshCore-like on cost.

\subsection{Unconditioned random-pair stress}
The ranking changes sharply without graph-selected recurring pairs.
Meshtastic-like obtains 0.708 ACK and 0.995 destination PDR at 38.9 s
aggregate TX airtime; MeshEcho obtains 0.486/0.515 at 152.8 s. Thus the
managed-flooding model dominates MeshEcho on both delivery metrics, airtime,
and modeled energy in this workload. Its pooled destination count is
809/813, versus MeshEcho's 415/813. This adverse result is not pooled with
the conditioned case. MeshEcho remains ahead of MeshCore-like by 0.105 ACK
PDR, but uses 4.40 s more airtime and 8.83 J more modeled energy per run.
The random case has 63.4\% of static direct links below 0.99 PRR; that
link statistic does not establish that every random pair has a viable
multi-hop route.

\subsection{Route-score and discovery checks}
The matched-recovery PRR-product control attains 0.621 versus MeshEcho's
0.624 ACK PDR on recurring pairs and 0.486 versus 0.486 on random pairs.
Both paired ACK intervals in Table~\ref{tab:paired} include zero, as do
their airtime intervals. The max-min score therefore has no demonstrated
advantage over this familiar metric under the tested workloads. In the
recurring case, MeshEcho and PRR+fb saw identical candidate sets for
81/83 paired discovery keys; in the random case they did so for only
449/804 keys. MeshEcho and MeshCore-like shared only 192/804 random-case
candidate sets. Even with matched request timing, subsequent interference
and route state differ. These full-run comparisons cannot isolate the
score or prove a particular candidate-ranking mechanism caused the ACK
gap versus MeshCore-like.

\subsection{Flow outcomes and route-state exposure}
Table~\ref{tab:events} counts events across all 20 seeds in each scenario.
``Destination only'' is a delivered DATA flow without a source-received
ACK, not a failed destination delivery. Its large value for the
Meshtastic-like model explains why the two PDR metrics tell different
stories. Recurring pairs generate hundreds of cache hits for routed
policies; random pairs generate only five across all runs. The random
workload therefore repeatedly pays discovery costs. MeshEcho records
551 successful discoveries among 807 attempts there, versus 494/807 for
MeshCore-like, but this is a complete-protocol difference, not a
controlled score comparison.

\subsection{Route-lifetime sensitivity}
The earlier 2.1.24 controls use the same current calibrated policy on the
recurring-pair seeds. With no temporal fading, MeshEcho reaches 0.717 ACK
PDR. Under 6-dB fading, shortening route TTL from 600 to 30 s changes
ACK PDR from 0.624 to 0.320 and aggregate TX airtime from 23.7 to
64.8 s. Paired 30-minus-600 s differences are $-0.304$ ACK PDR
($[-0.393,-0.215]$) and $+41.05$ s airtime
($[+31.40,+50.70]$). This changes the entire subsequent discovery and
interference history; it is a policy sensitivity check, not a controlled
estimate of one cache-aging mechanism or a reason to prefer longer TTLs
under real mobility.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The conditioned recurring case rewards a cached route and its ACK path;
the random case imposes repeated route discovery and includes potentially
unreachable pairs. These are different estimands, not two measurements of
one universal ranking. The random-case reversal is especially important:
the simplified flooding model is both more reliable and cheaper in
aggregate transmissions there. The design cannot support a blanket claim
that source routing always saves airtime or that MeshEcho outperforms
Meshtastic and MeshCore products.

For an application that requires a source-confirmed ACK on repeatedly
used, statically feasible pairs, MeshEcho's modeled trade-off is favorable
relative to these two simplified baselines. For sporadic random pairs,
the flooding model has the stronger result even on aggregate TX airtime.
If minimizing transmissions is primary in recurring pairs, the
MeshCore-like row is cheaper but loses delivery. These observations are
conditional comparisons, not deployment recommendations: changing the
traffic mix, channel, firmware routing state, or ACK procedure can change
the order.

\subsection{Protocol-model validity}
The two named baselines are deliberately stylized. Meshtastic 2.6+ can
learn next hops for direct messages; our model never does. MeshCore can
flood an initial message and learn from its delivery report; our model
requires a separate RREQ/RREP exchange before DATA. Real firmware also
has device roles, retry and duty-cycle behavior, routing-state details,
and packet formats not represented here. The reverse-path ACK modeled for
the Meshtastic-like flood is not a measured property of Meshtastic devices.
Consequently, these numbers compare algorithms in one harness, not actual
products. Fairness controls match traffic, PHY, cache TTL for routed
policies, and RREQ timing where applicable; they cannot equalize inherently
different packet exchanges or subsequent collision histories.

\subsection{Statistical and physical limits}
The 51--70 baseline comparisons reuse already-viewed holdout seeds, and
the new random-pair scenario was designed after that inspection. Nominal
paired CIs describe seed-to-seed variation but do not create confirmatory
evidence or correct the several exploratory contrasts. Twenty topology
seeds, rather than 793 or 813 flows, are the independent units. The
recurring case selects just four qualified pairs per seed and favors cache
reuse; the random case has no graph-feasibility gate. The two cases also
change area, so their absolute differences do not isolate pair scheduling.

The packet-level radio model includes path loss, shadowing, half-duplex
collisions, and capture, but not waveform interference, modem timing,
antenna changes, enforced regulatory duty cycle, or an empirical channel
trace. Modeled PRR is inferred from successfully received requests and
does not account for censored requests or measured packet losses. Aggregate
TX airtime is not channel-busy time, and the always-listening energy model
is not a battery measurement. A hardware or hardware-in-the-loop study with
actual firmware, packet traces, and measured energy is needed before
deployment claims. A separate, prospectively frozen scenario and a
fixed-candidate score test would be needed for stronger causal claims.

\section{Reproducibility}
Version 2.1.26 provides source, tests, per-seed CSVs, a recurring-pair
summary CSV, and reproduction commands for both four-policy experiments.
The raw CSVs contain application denominators and trace hashes plus
recorded discovery candidates for routed policies. The 51--70 calibrated
and PRR+fb rows reproduce the 2.1.24 holdout exactly; the additional
baselines and seeds 2001--2020 are new exploratory evidence. Numerical
claims were independently recomputed from the raw counts. No human
participants or personal data are involved. AI-assisted drafting and code
review were used.

\section{Conclusion}
In recurring connected conversations, MeshEcho's source-ACK delivery
exceeds both stylized baselines, but Meshtastic-like reaches far more
destinations and MeshCore-like uses less aggregate TX airtime. Under
unconditioned random demands, Meshtastic-like dominates MeshEcho in both
delivery metrics and cost. MeshEcho and matched-recovery PRR-product are
nearly identical in both cases. These results delimit where the current
simulated policy helps; they do not establish score novelty, firmware
superiority, or physical-network performance.
